Who's got the best economic mobility?

How long does it take a family at the bottom to climb to the middle — and is the climb getting easier? OECD estimates of the "social elevator," earnings persistence across generations, and four decades of income inequality, in three charts.

The verdict

Denmark has the best economic mobility of any country measured — two generations for a bottom-decile family to reach the middle, the weakest parent-to-child earnings persistence, and one of the lowest levels of income inequality. Norway, Finland and Sweden are right behind at three generations. The elevator is slowest in the large emerging economies: nine generations in Brazil and South Africa, eleven in Colombia. The United States and United Kingdom sit below the OECD average at five, despite their wealth — the "American Dream" gap between belief and measurement.

And the direction of travel is mostly the wrong way. Since the mid-1980s inequality has risen across most of the OECD — most sharply in the Nordics that started most equal (Sweden's Gini rose from ~0.20 to ~0.29) and in the US and UK, which started unequal and got more so. Because inequality and low mobility travel together (that's the Gatsby curve), rungs moving apart today tend to mean a slower elevator for the next generation. The stand-outs against the trend: France held flat for forty years, and Denmark rose the least of the Nordics — mobility's champion has also defended its lead.
